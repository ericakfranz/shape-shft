# Practice sessions

## Session lengths

Offer about 5, 10, 15 or 20 minutes. These are suggestions. If the user gets absorbed in a useful conversation with themselves and keeps going, that is good.

## Support levels

| | Guided (default for beginners) | Supported | Independent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Context | Full explanation of the job and the facts | Short summary of the facts | Short memory cues |
| Structure | Small numbered steps | Flexible steps | Broad topics, little structure |
| Sentence starters | Several per step | A few | None, unless asked |
| Follow-up prompts | Specific prompts for every step | Several per topic | Optional |
| Example wording | Optional full examples, clearly marked as examples | Optional | None |
| Reminders to speak | In every step | At the start and at key points | At the start |
| When stuck | Concrete help to keep going | A hint | Recall exercises |

The level can differ by topic. Record it in the Practice Journal, for example "Technical work: Independent. Leadership: Guided."

## Building a session

A beginner session usually includes these parts. Use only the parts that fit the user's time, confidence and goal.

1. Specific context: the job or situation.
2. Why the topic matters, in one or two sentences.
3. A clear invitation to start speaking out loud.
4. An easy warm-up.
5. A specific experience to explore.
6. The facts to remember about it (from the Career Profile).
7. Sentence starters.
8. Follow-up prompts.
9. Exploring reasoning and decisions.
10. Exploring outcomes and numbers, where they exist.
11. Connecting the experience to the opportunity.
12. Optional: a shorter retelling.
13. Reflection, and how to bring discoveries back to the coach.

Rules:
- Only use facts from the Career Profile. If a fact is `[to clarify]`, say so in the session and suggest the user think about it.
- Example wording is illustrative. Label it: "Example only. Use your own words."
- Repeat the instruction to speak out loud inside the steps.
- For walk sessions, add the walk note and the "stop somewhere safe to read" note.

## Conversational bridges

Teach these as tools for thinking and speaking, not scripts. Include them in beginner materials:

- "The reason that mattered was..."
- "What led me to that decision was..."
- "What made that especially hard was..."
- "What that meant for the business (or the customers, the team, the patients) was..."
- "My part in it was..."
- "Looking back, I think..."
- "Another way I could have handled it was..."
- "That reminds me of another time when..."
- "Something I want to do more of is..."
- "The main point I'm trying to make is..."

## Recovery skills

Teach the user to:

- pause without apologizing ("Let me think about that for a second" is fine)
- restart a story from a different point
- come back to the main point after a tangent
- ask themselves a follow-up question out loud ("Why did that matter?")
- say an explanation a different way
- notice when a listener needs more context

If someone struggles to speak for even thirty seconds, start smaller: finishing simple sentences, describing a familiar place at work, or describing what a normal morning looked like. Meet the person where they are.

## Exercises

Mix these. Favor active recall, explanation, flexible use and spacing over re-reading.

- **Finish the sentence:** say a starter and keep going.
- **Describe a scene:** a specific place, day or moment at work, with details.
- **Explain the job:** describe your work to someone who knows nothing about it.
- **Think out loud:** talk through a decision as you remember making it.
- **Blank-page recall:** talk about a story before looking at the facts, then check what you left out.
- **Teach it:** explain a skill or concept to an imaginary new coworker.
- **Explore a number:** where it came from, what it means, who it affected.
- **Business impact:** explain why the work mattered to the organization.
- **Change the listener:** tell the same story to a recruiter, a future teammate, a senior leader, a friend.
- **Change the angle:** the same story focused on the problem, on the people, on the decision, or on the lesson.
- **Connect two experiences** that seem unrelated.
- **Follow a tangent** for a minute, then come back to the point.
- **Summarize:** after a long exploration, tell it in one or two minutes, then again in different words.
- **Respectful disagreement:** explain a professional opinion and the strongest argument against it.
- **Other choices:** what else you could have done, and why you did not.
- **Recover from silence:** stop for five seconds on purpose, then continue.
- **Voice journal** (optional): record yourself on your phone and listen back, if that helps you.
- **Random topic:** the user asks you for a random topic from their story bank.
- **Spaced revisit:** return to a story practiced a few days ago, from a new angle.

## Example guided session

This example uses a fictional candidate (Morgan Ellery, in the ShapeShft examples) who applied for an Operations Manager role. Use it as a model for structure and tone. Replace every fact with the user's own.

> **Today's practice: How you find and solve problems at work**
> About 15 to 20 minutes.
>
> The Operations Manager job emphasizes improving processes and reducing waste. Today, explore the receiving process you improved at Cedar Ridge.
>
> If you can, take this practice outside for an easy walk. Keep this guide open and look at it whenever you need another idea. Stop somewhere safe before you read.
>
> The exercise is to talk out loud to yourself. You do not need to sound polished.
>
> **Step 1: Get yourself talking**
> Imagine you are describing Cedar Ridge to someone who has never heard of it. Start speaking out loud. If you are stuck, finish one of these sentences:
> - "The company I worked for was responsible for..."
> - "On a normal day, I would..."
> - "One thing that made my job interesting was..."
> - "Something that used to frustrate me was..."
>
> Keep talking as thoughts come. You do not need to finish every sentence or go in order.
>
> **Step 2: Go back to a specific experience**
> Here is what you told us:
> - Inventory counts did not match, which delayed store orders.
> - You looked into how freight was received.
> - Each shift received freight differently.
> - You wrote a standard checklist.
> - You trained six coworkers to use it.
> - Discrepancies dropped by about 40% over six months.
>
> Instead of repeating these facts, describe what the situation was like. Read one prompt, then talk out loud about whatever it brings to mind:
> - What did a bad day look like when the counts were wrong?
> - Who was affected?
> - Is there one incident that stands out?
> - When did you first realize something had to change?
> - Why did you decide to get involved?
>
> If you can't get started, try saying: "I remember there was this problem with inventory that kept coming back. At the time I don't think we really understood why, but what I noticed was..." Then keep going however feels natural.
>
> **Step 3: Explore how you think**
> Talk through your decisions. If you run out of things to say, pick a prompt:
> - Why did you look at the receiving process first?
> - What else could you have tried?
> - Why did a checklist make sense?
> - How did your coworkers react?
> - What surprised you?
> - What would you do differently today?
>
> Follow interesting tangents. It does not need to sound like an interview answer.
>
> **Step 4: Get comfortable with the result**
> Discrepancies dropped by about 40%. Spend a few minutes talking about that.
> - How did you know things had improved?
> - What did the improvement look like on a normal day?
> - Why did it matter, and who benefited?
> - What did you do yourself?
> - What else might have helped the result?
>
> If you don't remember exactly how the number was measured, say that. Don't make up an explanation. Note it, and bring it back to your coach.
>
> **Step 5: Connect it to the new job**
> Imagine you have started as Operations Manager at Larkspur. Talk about how this experience would shape your approach. Try:
> - "One thing I've learned about improving a process is..."
> - "Before I change anything, I like to understand..."
> - "Something I'd want to learn about Larkspur's operation is..."
>
> Talk about what kind of manager you want to be.
>
> **Step 6: Tell the story again**
> Someone is genuinely interested, but you only have a minute or two. What would you keep? What could you leave out? Tell the story out loud. Then tell it again in different words. Don't memorize a version.
>
> **Step 7: Reflect**
> - Did you remember anything you had forgotten?
> - What was easy to talk about? Where did you get stuck?
> - Did you notice anything about how you approach work?
> - What do you want to explore next?
>
> Bring what you noticed back to your coach when it's convenient.

## After the session

When the user returns:

1. Ask what happened, briefly: what was easy, where they got stuck, what they remembered.
2. Confirm any new fact and offer to add it to the Career Profile.
3. If they want refinement, help with one thing at a time, and ask them to say the new version out loud.
4. Suggest the next session, using the Practice Journal's "Topics to revisit".
5. Update the Practice Journal.
