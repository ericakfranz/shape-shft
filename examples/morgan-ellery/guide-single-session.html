How to use this guide

The practice is talking out loud to yourself. Read a prompt, then start speaking. Reading, thinking or writing alone is not the exercise.

  • You do not need to sound polished. Stumbling, pausing, repeating yourself and going off on tangents are all part of it.
  • Keep talking even if you are not sure where the story is going yet.
  • If you lose your train of thought, stop for a moment, then keep going. There is a list of ways to restart at the end.
  • Keep this guide open and look at it as often as you need. You do not need to remember any of it.
  • If you can, take the practice outside for an easy walk somewhere you feel comfortable talking to yourself. Stop somewhere safe before you read your phone. Practicing at home, sitting or standing, works just as well.
  • Afterward, tell your AI coach what you remembered and where you got stuck.

Today's plan

The Operations Manager job at Larkspur lists "own inventory accuracy" and "find and lead continuous improvement projects". Today, explore the receiving checklist you created at Cedar Ridge.

Step 1: Get yourself talking

Imagine you are describing Cedar Ridge to someone who has never heard of it. Start speaking out loud. If you are having trouble getting started, finish any of these sentences:

"The company I worked for was responsible for..."

"On a normal day on second shift, I would..."

"One thing that made my job interesting was..."

"Something that used to frustrate me was..."

Keep talking as thoughts come to you. You do not need to finish every sentence or go in order.

Step 2: Go back to a specific experience

Here is what you told your coach:

  • Inventory counts did not match the shelves, so store orders went out short.
  • You watched receiving on all three shifts for two weeks.
  • Every shift received freight differently.
  • You wrote a one-page checklist.
  • You trained six receivers to use it.
  • Discrepancies dropped by about 40% over six months (roughly 50 a week down to roughly 30, your estimate).

Instead of repeating these facts, describe what it was like to be there. Read one prompt, then talk out loud about whatever it brings to mind:

  • What did a bad day look like when the counts were wrong?
  • Who was affected: stores, drivers, your crew?
  • Is there one incident that stands out?
  • When did you first realize something had to change?
  • Why did you decide to get involved, when it was not formally your job?

If you are struggling to begin, try saying:

"I remember there was this problem with inventory that kept coming back. At the time I don't think we really understood why, but what I noticed was..."

Continue that sentence in whatever way feels natural.

Step 3: Explore how you think

Talk through your decisions and your reasoning. If you run out of things to say, pick a prompt:

  • Why did you watch all three shifts before changing anything?
  • Why a paper checklist and not a new system?
  • How did the receivers react at first?
  • What surprised you?
  • What would you do differently today? (You told your coach: involve the other shift leads from the start.)

Follow any interesting tangents. It does not need to sound like an interview answer.

Step 4: Get comfortable with the result

Discrepancies dropped by about 40%. Spend a few minutes talking out loud about that result.

  • How did you know things had improved? (The inventory control manager's weekly report.)
  • What did the improvement look like on a normal shift?
  • Why did it matter to the stores?
  • What did you do yourself, and what did others do?
  • What else might have helped the numbers?

If you are not sure exactly how the report counted discrepancies, say so out loud. Do not invent an explanation. Bring the question back to your coach.

Step 5: Connect it to Larkspur

Imagine you have started as Operations Manager at the Fairmont Junction distribution center. It opened in 2023 and is still growing. Talk about how your Cedar Ridge experience would shape your approach.

"One thing I've learned about improving a process is..."

"Before I change anything, I like to understand..."

"Something I'd want to learn about Larkspur's operation is..."

Then talk about what kind of manager you want to be for the two shift supervisors.

Step 6: Tell the story again

You have now spent several minutes exploring this experience. Imagine someone is genuinely interested, but you only have a minute or two.

  • What would you keep?
  • What could you leave out?

Tell the story out loud. Then tell it again in different words. Do not memorize a version.

Example only. Use your own words.

At Cedar Ridge our store orders kept going out short because the records didn't match the shelves. I spent two weeks watching how each shift received freight and found that everyone did it a little differently. So I wrote a one-page checklist and trained six receivers on it. Within about six months the weekly discrepancies were down around 40 percent, and the other shifts started using it too.

If you get stuck

Pick any of these and say it out loud. Then keep going. They are tools for thinking, not scripts.

  • "The reason that mattered was..."
  • "What led me to that decision was..."
  • "What made that especially hard was..."
  • "What that meant for the people involved was..."
  • "My part in it was..."
  • "Looking back, I think..."
  • "Another way I could have handled it was..."
  • "That reminds me of another time when..."
  • "The main point I am trying to make is..."

Ways to restart

  • Pause. Take a breath. You do not need to apologize.
  • Start the story again from a different point, for example from the end.
  • Ask yourself a question out loud: "Why did that matter?"
  • Describe one small detail: a place, a person, a sound, a tool.
  • Say the same idea in different words.
